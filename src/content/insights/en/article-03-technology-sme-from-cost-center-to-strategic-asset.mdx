In an SME, a technology project that goes off track cuts into profit margin. Of 5,360 projects studied, 18% overran their budget by more than half, and those cost on average 5.5 times what was planned ([*Project Management Journal*, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/87569728251340590)). These overruns drain available cash. On the day of a sale, a buyer who sees a recurring risk deducts it from the price.

In an established company, sales answers for growth, operations for margin and finance for cash. For what technology costs and what it returns, more often than not, no one answers. When a project costs five times the planned amount, is it simply an estimation problem, or rather a governance problem?

In many SMEs, the leadership table comes down to the owner, the controller and the operations manager. One of them still has to answer for technology.

## IT as a cost centre: a reasonable but incomplete answer

Which function should technology report to in an SME? The most common answer fits in one sentence: IT is a routine expense, administrative in nature, that reports to finance or operations. That is what job postings for IT director or vice-president roles show. It is also the answer most executives give me when I ask.

That answer is reasonable. Technology is not tied to the company's core mission. Its costs show up every month, while its benefits are diffuse. Under those circumstances, the reflex to manage it like rent or insurance is understandable.

Yet it has a weakness: technology then reports to someone else. Placed under finance, it is often subject to fierce cost control, even cost cutting, at the expense of the long-term vision. Placed under operations, it is consumed by daily emergencies, and medium-term planning takes a back seat.

With no one at the leadership table to answer for technology, no one has the mandate to weigh budget constraints, operational needs and business vision against one another. In practice, the company tracks what its technology costs, but lacks the information it needs to confirm or refute that it pays off.

## What sets apart the SMEs that gain an advantage from their technology

The SMEs that gain an advantage from their technology stand out in how they decide. According to a research review conducted at Corvinus University of Budapest, they rely on a strategy suited to their reality, committed leadership, a gradual approach and skills development, rather than on complex and costly investments ([*Information Systems and e-Business Management*, 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10257-024-00682-2)).

Canadian data point the same way. According to BDC, 96% of SMEs invested in digital technology in 2025. If all of them reached the digital maturity of the most advanced 8%, their productivity could rise by up to 38% ([BDC, 2026](https://www.bdc.ca/en/about/mediaroom/news-releases/350b-opportunity-canadas-next-phase-of-growth-to-be-driven-by-ai-and-digital-technologies)). And 99% of digitally advanced businesses have a digital plan ([BDC, 2022](https://www.bdc.ca/en/about/mediaroom/news-releases/small-medium-sized-businesses-that-invest-digital-technologies-more-competitive-bdc-study)). **The gap between SMEs that spend and those that gain an advantage therefore comes down to the rigour of the decision, far more than to the amount invested.**

My experience points the same way: I have seen operational efficiency gains of 30% to 50% in highly manual processes, still on paper, once they were digitized and automated, sometimes with the help of AI.

At the company level, this return takes two forms. Well deployed, technology brings efficiency and quality. Well conceived, it creates differentiation and new sources of revenue. Yet the IT budget lumps together expenses that play these different roles. I recommend sorting them into three categories, each with its own objective and treatment.

| Category | Examples | Objective | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core operational needs | Infrastructure, email, basic accounting, security, maintenance | Standardize and reduce costs | Predictable operating expense |
| Efficiency drivers | ERP, CRM, logistics automation | Work like the well-run companies in your industry, without reinventing the wheel | Business case based on margin or working capital |
| What sets you apart from your competitors | Intellectual property, a quote configurator, a customer portal, your production data | Build a competitive advantage | Strict governance and independent architecture review |

Only the first category is a routine expense. The other two are investments, and a company that manages them all as a cost centre forgoes the return on the last two.

## What a poorly governed IT investment costs

A poorly governed technology investment costs far more than its budget. The opening figures come from the work of Bent Flyvbjerg, Alexander Budzier and their co-authors. In their data, the median project stays on budget. The risk is concentrated in a minority of projects whose costs spiral.

The finding has held for fifteen years. As early as 2011, the same researchers found, across 1,471 projects, that one project in six had an average overrun of 200% ([*Harvard Business Review*, 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.0265)). In 2026, they compare IT with 22 other project types: it is the only one of the 23 categories exposed to what they describe as infinite, unpredictable risk. They put forward two explanations: reliance on custom builds rather than modular components, and planning decisions made too quickly.

I see nothing in this mechanism that is specific to large companies. An SME simply has less room to absorb it. For a company with $5M in revenue, a new $150,000 ERP that fell into this group would cost more than $800,000, or about one-sixth of annual revenue.

A project does not quintuple in cost overnight. Between the first overrun and the last, management authorized the work to continue several times. **The initial estimate explains the starting point, and every subsequent authorization is a governance decision.**

## How technical debt moves from the code to the balance sheet and the income statement

A bad technology investment keeps costing long after go-live. One analogy is enough to see what is at stake. A building bought without an inspection sits on the balance sheet at its purchase price, hidden defects included. The same goes for software.

These hidden defects have a name. Technical debt refers to all the design shortcuts taken to deliver faster, whose cost is carried over to every future change. Each new feature has to work around earlier compromises. Dependencies between components multiply, and the researchers use them to explain extreme overruns: a problem in a single component can set off a chain reaction in the components that depend on it.

Technical debt extends beyond the code. Weak governance and immature IT processes add hidden costs and risks: undocumented architecture decisions, dependence on a single person or vendor, known security flaws left unpatched, disaster recovery plans never tested.

According to McKinsey, technical debt accounts for about 40% of companies' technology estate, and they pay 10% to 20% more on every project to address it ([McKinsey, 2023](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-digital/our-insights/breaking-technical-debts-vicious-cycle-to-modernize-your-business)).

### On the balance sheet: an asset valued at what it cost

If your company capitalizes its technology development, show this passage to your controller or CPA. Software on the balance sheet is worth what it cost, not what it earns (ASPE Section 3064). If it has to be replaced earlier than planned, the remaining value is written off at once and hits that year's results.

The problem gets worse when capitalization is used mainly to lighten the current year's budget. **The amortization deferred in this way reduces earnings in the following years and limits the company's capacity to invest, when the time comes, at the level a major transformation project requires.**

### On the income statement and cash flow: constant friction

A poor technology fit most often shows up as daily friction, far more than as a major outage. A poorly configured ERP or a CRM disconnected from billing delays invoicing, and therefore collections, and inflates working capital needs. Meanwhile, integration gaps between systems are bridged by hand, by administrative payroll that appears in no IT budget.

## Technical debt and business valuation: a governance issue

What looked like an IT budget problem changes nature the day the company is put up for sale or prepares for succession. During due diligence, the buyer examines the systems the way they examine the financial statements. An aging, poorly documented or single-vendor-dependent system forces the buyer to plan for investments after the transaction, and the buyer factors that into the offer. The buyer also assesses the maturity of IT processes and the quality of governance, and treats any gaps as a risk. The deadline is close for many: according to CFIB, 76% of Canadian SME owners plan to exit their business within ten years, and only 9% have a formal succession plan ([CFIB, 2023](https://cfib-fcei.ca/en/media/over-2-trillion-in-business-assets-are-at-stake-as-majority-of-small-business-owners-plan-to-exit-their-business-over-the-next-decade)).

At that point, the question goes beyond technology. Most vendors sell their technology or their delivery teams. Their commercial incentives are entirely legitimate, but their recommendation cannot be neutral. **Someone at the leadership table must defend the company's interests exclusively and act as a trusted third party between the company and its vendors.**

Some will object that an SME with 30 or 150 employees is not large enough to hire a full-time IT director, and the objection is valid. However, governance depends on having a competent, independent voice when decisions are made, and a part-time mandate is enough to provide it. That is the role of a [fractional CTO](/en/services/sme/).

## Five checks before any major IT investment

Before any major commitment, five checks are essential:

- **The business case.** Quantify the expected margin gain, cost reduction or risk mitigation.
- **The commercial product.** Show why an off-the-shelf commercial product falls short before funding custom development.
- **The way out.** Assess what it would cost to switch vendors and recover your data.
- **The total cost of ownership** (everything the system will cost over three to five years). Set the budget accordingly, including maintenance, security and change management.
- **An independent perspective.** Name the person who defends the company's interests with vendors: an internal executive, or an advisor who sells nothing.

## Where to start: measure before you invest

These five checks apply from your next project onward, with no outside help. One simple measure can even come first: list the company's systems and note, next to each one, its annual cost, the category it belongs to and who answers for it in the company. An empty column is already information.

Which system to tackle first and how much technical debt there is then depend on your systems, your contracts, your financial statements and the projects already under way. An [IT maturity assessment](/en/services/sme/) is how to build that picture.
